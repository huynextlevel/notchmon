How long have you been sitting

Two clocks, not one. Desk is time at the machine, measured from input idle, the lock flag and the display — it keeps counting while you read documentation in a browser, because your back does not care whether an agent was running. Coding is the part of it with an agent alive, and it is always a subset.

Chosen: A · Readout, with the Overview band and the Time tab as drawn. B and C are kept below as the record of what was weighed.

Every figure on this page is measured from 98 real sessions on this machine. The strip already carries quota on the left and tokens on the right, so a third thing has to be paid for. These are three ways to pay.

A

Readout

The stretch is a figure on the strip, always. You glance up and you know. It shifts to amber at 60 minutes and to red at 90, where it also starts a slow pulse. The spend figure is dropped to pay for the room.

You always know, without asking. The cost is two things: the spend figure leaves the strip, and a number that is always there is a number you watch. A clock counting up in the corner of your eye is its own small pressure — which for some people is the point, and for others is the reason they turn it off.

B

Tide

No number anywhere on the strip. The strip's own bottom edge is the gauge: a hairline that fills from the left as the stretch runs out, amber at 60 minutes, red and breathing past 90. Nothing is added, nothing is displaced.

Costs nothing and displaces nothing — the only variant where quota, tokens and spend all survive untouched. It also asks nothing of you: a line you never read is a line you can ignore for hours. And it cannot be precise: you feel "a while", you never learn "1h47m" without opening the panel.

C

Nudge

Silent all the way to the threshold. At 90 minutes the strip waits for the next Stop — the instant an agent hands control back, before you have started typing again — and only then puts a lozenge up. It is the cheapest moment in the whole working day to be interrupted, and no clock-driven timer can find it.

Silent by default, and it picks its moment. The cost is that you have no sense of the hour building — the first you hear of it is being told. And it collides with the session lozenge: if an agent needs you at the same instant, two lozenges want the same slot. The agent has to win — something is blocked on you — so the rest waits for the Stop after that.

The range governs the chart and the four figures under it, not the chart alone. All four are window-scoped — "days with work on them" has no meaning without a window — so a control that moved only the bars would leave four numbers below it silently answering a different question.

Today is a different question, so it gets different figures. "31 of 31 days" collapses to "1 of 1", and the median of one day is that day. What a single day can say instead: how long the longest sit ran, how many separate sits there were, and when you started and were last seen. The chart marks the busiest hour rather than a peak — a peak across one day is just the tallest of six bars.

Ranges longer than the history are not offered. On a machine that has been tracking for four days, 30D and All are the same four days; the segment stays visible but goes inactive rather than promising a month that does not exist. Silently showing four days under a label that says 30D is the version of this that lies.

The rhythm chart is the reason the tab exists. Everything else here is a total, and a total can live in a band on Overview. The shape of a day cannot — and this one says something a total never would: the peak is 21:00, and 24% of all work happens between 22:00 and 06:00. Hours 03:00 to 07:00 are the only ones that are genuinely empty.

Night hours are marked twice — a shifted bar colour and a rule beneath them — because a band told apart by hue alone is a band some readers cannot see at all.

B and C compose. The tide gives the hour a shape you can feel without reading, and the nudge is what happens when feeling it was not enough. A is the one that has to win alone, because it is the only one that spends strip width every minute of every day — and the only one that puts a running clock in your eyeline while you work.
