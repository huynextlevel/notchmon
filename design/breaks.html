How it interrupts

Three sizes of the same nudge, on the same eleven pixels. Inline changes nothing but the mark. Pill grows the black shape itself — the notch is already a black rounded rectangle, so it can open a little without becoming a window. Panel is the whole thing dropping down. Press fire the nudge to watch a threshold pass: it opens, plays three times, and closes itself.

Drawn at true size — the notch is 200pt across and 32pt deep on a 14-inch, and the strip is 26pt. The camera keeps its 58pt of clearance in every size, which is why nothing sits in the middle.

The eleven

Each one loops on a stepped clock — no tweening. Pixel art moves in whole frames or it stops looking like pixel art. Cycle times are deliberately slow: this sits in peripheral vision all day, and a fast loop is a thing you have to look away from.

Rules these follow

The mascot keeps its job

Six of the eleven are the notchling that already lives in that slot, doing something else — yawning, stretching, walking. A creature the user knows, behaving differently, reads faster than a new object appearing. The other five are things, not characters: a mug, a glass, an eye.

Colour is never the message

Every sprite is one palette token — control, caution, critical, or night — plus its own 38% shade. So they re-tint with the theme, and none of them invents a hue. Saturated colour in this app belongs to a vendor; a green sprite would read as an agent.

Ambient, then a nudge

Same sprite, two speeds. Under the threshold it idles slowly and says nothing. At the threshold it runs the full loop three times and settles. A reminder that plays forever is a reminder that gets switched off.

Reduced motion is a still frame

The strip already honours accessibilityReduceMotion by dropping back to the mark. A break nudge cannot drop back to nothing, so it holds frame 0 — the picture still says coffee, it just doesn't steam. Your system asks for reduced motion — that is what you are seeing above.
